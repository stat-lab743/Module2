A psychology research team is evaluating average attention scores in three hypothetical studies. The reference population has μ = 50 and a known σ = 12. Each study reports M = 54, but the studies have different sample sizes. The team wants to understand how unusual each result would be if the population mean were 50.

Assume independent random observations from a normal population. This lets us use normal sampling distributions even for Study A’s small sample. The sample mean M summarizes a group; it is not an individual participant’s score.

Concept connection

A sampling distribution describes the means we would obtain from repeated random samples of the same size. Its standard deviation is the standard error. Here, SE = σ / √n, and z = (M − μ) / SE measures distance from the reference mean in standard error units. For nonnormal populations with finite variance, the central limit theorem explains why the distribution of means approaches normal as sample size increases. Our population is assumed normal, so the sampling distribution is normal for all three sample sizes.

A larger z magnitude indicates a result farther from the reference mean relative to expected sampling variation. This does not tell us the probability that the population mean is 50, or prove what caused a difference.
